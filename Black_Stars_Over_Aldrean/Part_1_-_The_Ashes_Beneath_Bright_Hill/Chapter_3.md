## Chapter 3 - The Prison Below




He came back to himself in pieces.

Not all at once, the way waking from ordinary sleep worked, but incrementally and in the wrong order.
Sound arrived first.
A low, sustained metallic resonance, as if the air itself was under tension, held to some frequency that did not quite resolve into a note and did not quite stop being one.
Then cold, which was everywhere and came from no particular direction.
Then pain, which had been patient.

He was lying on stone.

Not the gritty composite of battlefield ground but dressed and level stone, placed deliberately and doing its job without complaint beneath him.
He opened his eyes.

The ceiling was low and black.
Black stone, close-fitted, with thin lines of mortar running in precise grids overhead.
No crystalline growth along the seams.
No settling cracks.
It simply was, the way certain things seemed simply to have always been.
The light arrived from a corridor beyond a locked iron door, filtering through the small barred window set into its upper half.
Torchlight.
It moved the way torchlight should, pulling in the draft, but something about it was wrong at the edges of his attention in a way he could not immediately name.

He tried to sit up.

His left arm communicated its condition clearly and without ambiguity.
He stopped.
He lay very still and took careful stock: ribs on the right side, at least two, possibly three.
The cut along his flank.
His legs mostly untouched, which told him they wanted him mobile at some point.

He sat up using only his right arm.

The cell was small.
Perhaps twelve feet by eight.
A floor drain in the far corner with a mineral crust around its rim.
Two narrow sleeping pallets, both thin, one occupied by a woman who was watching him from across the room with the calm and unhurried attention of someone who had been watching for some time and had not found it necessary to announce herself.

---

She had white hair and violet-grey eyes and she was sitting with her back against the far wall and her knees drawn up.
She looked at him without any particular alarm, which was either very calm of her or the result of how long she had been in this room.

She appeared roughly his age, though something in the way she held herself made that estimate feel uncertain.
Her stillness did not look like the stillness of someone who had simply run out of things to do with her hands.
It looked practiced, the kind of composure that had been taught rather than found, though he could not have said where that impression came from or why he trusted it.
Thin build.
Pale skin.
Layered robes that had been elegant once, now worn through at the elbows, the decorative border at the cuffs reduced to a faint impression of itself.
Symbols worked into the fabric at intervals.
Not very decorative.

She let him finish looking around the cell.
She seemed to understand this was a necessary process and did not interrupt it.

"Three days," she began, when his gaze returned to her.
Her voice was even and calibrated precisely to the room's size, no louder than necessary.
"You were feverish for the first two.
You said several things."

"What things."

"Names.
One in particular, quite a lot."
A brief pause.
"I didn't write them down."

He looked at his left arm.
The sleeve had been cut away.
What remained below the elbow was bound tightly in cloth that wasn't the ribbon Aella had tied there, which was gone.
The binding was functional rather than careful, the kind done in haste with available material.
He tested the seam with two fingers.
It held.

"You re-bound it," he noted.

"Twice.
The first time held for about six hours."
She unfolded her legs and stretched them out, the movement of someone making herself comfortable now that the preliminary phase of the conversation was clearly over.
"The second time I did it tighter and it has held since yesterday."

He looked at the door.
Iron.
Heavy.
A lock he could see from here, and no handle on this side.

"How long have you been in this cell," he asked.

She considered the question.
"Long enough that I stopped keeping a reliable count."
She looked at the door too, briefly.
"Time inside the Hold does not move the way it should.
You'll notice it.
Most people notice the torches first."

He looked back at the barred window in the door.
The torch visible in the corridor beyond it was barely diminished from what it must have been hours ago.
Burning too slowly.
The flame moving at the right speed while the fuel consumed at the wrong one.

*There it is.*

"It gets worse the deeper into the building you go," she continued.
"The lower levels are nearly unusable for measuring anything.
Guards rotate out of them early.
Some of them refuse entirely."
She looked at her hands, turning them over once.
"Wounds heal strangely down here.
Some faster than they should.
Some slower.
It's not consistent.
I haven't found the logic yet."

"You're looking for  logic behind it?"

"I'm always looking for the logic," she answered, and there was something in it that was not quite humor and not quite resignation.
Something that had been one or the other a long time ago and had settled into a territory between them.
"I am Aife."

"Denji."

She nodded as though she had already known and was simply confirming.
"I know.
The guards talked about your arrival.
They talk more than they realize."
She looked at him steadily.
"You survived things on the transit here that most people don't survive."

"So I've been told."

"You don't say that with as much curiosity as I would expect."

He looked at the door again.
"Where are we, exactly."

"Lower intake level.
Four stories below the entry chambers.
The guards call this section the Quiet Tier."
She let that sit for a moment.
"Which tells you something about what they think of the sections below it."

The metallic resonance in the air shifted slightly.
Not louder, not softer.
Just different, briefly, like a chord that had been struck somewhere far below and was still deciding what it was going to become.
He had been aware of it since he woke and had not put words to it yet.
He wasn't sure words were the right instrument for it.

"It comes from below," he murmured.

"Yes."

"How far below."

She looked at the floor.
"Further than the guards will go."

---

He learned her name but it took longer to learn her.

She was not unfriendly, was the thing.
He had expected detachment, the flattened effect of someone who had been in a small room alone for too long, but she was sharper than that, quicker.
She asked him questions about Bright Hill with the specific hunger of someone who had been cut off from information and missed it badly.
Not gossip.
Information.
She wanted to know about the Matrix’s council structure, about what scholars had published recently, about whether the sealed sections of the Citadel’s lower levels had been opened.

It struck him, distantly, as an odd set of things for a scholar to want to know first.
Not the histories.
The structure of who currently held power and how.
He did not remark on it.
He simply noted it, the way he noted most things, and set it aside.
He answered what he could, which wasn’t everything she asked, and she accepted the gaps without pushing.

She told him, on the second day, that he was the first person to share this cell in a long time.

She offered it plainly.
Not as a bid for sympathy, just as a piece of context being offered.
"The one before you was a man from the eastern settlements.
He had been here about a month when I arrived."
She turned a loose thread from her robe's hem between her fingers.
"He was a practical person.
Good company.
He died of the fever about six weeks ago."
She paused.
"The guards removed him and did not replace him for quite some time."

Denji offered nothing.

"I'm not telling you this so you feel sorry for me," she went on, which was exactly what he had thought she might say.
"I'm telling you because it explains why I talked more in the first two days than I perhaps should have.
You were feverish and couldn't hear most of it."
A brief, dry thing in her expression.
"Consider it a rehearsal."

He looked at the ceiling.
"What did you talk about."

"History, mostly.
That is usually what I talk about when no one can argue with me."

“Where are you from,” he asked, mostly to keep the quiet from settling back in.

“Far enough that it doesn’t matter anymore.”
She said it lightly, already moving past it.
“Tell me something about Bright Hill instead.
I’ve used up most of what I know.”

He let it go.
It did not occur to him yet that she had done this before, smoothly enough that he would not think to count it until much later.

---

She told him about the Black Hole on the third day.

Not from rumor the way the other prisoners moved their information around in pieces, trading partial knowledge through the gap under their doors or through the walls when the guards were far enough away.
She told him from something closer to scholarship, the measured and careful voice of a person who had assembled facts across a long time and was presenting the result.

The Hold had been built around it.
Not above it, not beside it: around it.
Whoever had drawn the original plans had known exactly what was in the lower levels and had constructed the entire structure as a containment apparatus first, with everything else, the prison, the coliseum, and the laboratory levels.
Appended later by people who understood the structure as a building rather than what it actually was.

“A seal,” Denji murmured.

She looked at him for a moment before answering.
“It’s the closest word I’ve found for it, in what little I’ve been able to read.”
She paused.
“I’ve never seen a source confirm it outright. I could have the shape of it wrong.”
She studied his face for a moment.
“How did you arrive at that.”

"I don't know," he admitted, which was true.
The word had arrived with a certainty he hadn't earned.
He had been looking at the floor when he spoke it, thinking about the crystalline roots he had seen through the cracked earth on the battlefield.
The conviction that they had run all the way here.
"What is it sealing."

Aife was quiet for a moment.
Not composing a lie.
Deciding whether a particular truth was the right one to give, and in what order to give it.
"Something that does not have a clean name in any language I have studied," she replied.
"The prisoners call it the Black Hole, which is not wrong but is not complete.
The guards who have been on the lower levels call it other things.
None of the names describe it accurately because names work by comparison, and there is nothing to compare it to."
She looked at the floor where he had been looking.
"What I can tell you is that it predates the Hold.
It predates recorded history.
The structure around it was built by people who understood what it was and considered containment the only viable response."

"What happened to those people."

"They're gone," she answered.
"Long gone.
Whatever they built here, it wasn't enough to save them."

The metallic sound moved through the air again.
Long and low and attentive.

---

He heard Mei before he saw her.

Two sets of guards' footsteps, moving fast, and between them a third set that was not cooperating.
Not panicked resistance, nothing desperate.
The deliberate, costly kind, the kind that said: *I know this won't work and I'm doing it anyway because the alternative is letting you think it will always be easy.*

They came past the door.

Through the barred window he caught a clear look at her for about four seconds.
Black hair cut unevenly at the jaw.
Sharp amber eyes that found his through the bars with an immediacy that suggested she had already been watching the door.
Thin.
Prison-thin, the kind that happened over months.
Her expression when she met his eyes was not a plea.
It was not fear.
It was something self-contained and undefeated, a look that said: *yes, and what of it.*

Then she was past and gone down the corridor.

"She came in before you," Aife told him from behind.
"About a week before.
She went to the laboratory levels on her second day and came back different."
Her voice was careful and flat.
"She went back again this morning."

Denji stayed at the door.
"Different how."

"Quieter.
Then not quiet at all.
Then quiet again but not in the same way as before."
A pause.
"There is a woman who runs the laboratory levels.
She is called Kiana.
What she does to people down there she describes as necessary.
What it looks like from outside is something else."

He looked down the corridor through the bars.
It was empty.
The distant sound of a second door, and then nothing.

"What does she want with the prisoners."

"She wants to know what they can become," Aife replied.
"She is not particularly interested in what they are."

He stayed at the door for a while longer.
He was not certain what he was waiting for.
Eventually he went back and sat against the wall.

*The look in that woman's eyes was not the look of someone who feared her jailer.*

He kept coming back to that.
There was something in it that sat wrong.
Familiarity that had curdled into something, hatred or grief or both, but familiarity underneath.
He had seen it before, on faces in the Citadel, in the particular way people looked at those they had once trusted and no longer did.
It was a specific expression.
It required history.

He did not say this to Aife.
He filed it away with the torches and the metallic sound and the roots beneath the earth.

---

Kiana came at the end of the fourth day.

He heard her footsteps on the stone first, a measured rhythm with no variation at all, the stride of someone who had long since stopped caring about appearing unhurried because they simply were.
She appeared in the barred window of the door and looked at him with the attention one gave to a document being assessed for authenticity.

She was elegant.
That was the first word, and it was accurate in the way a weapon's description could be accurate.
Long dark hair, worn back without ornament.
Pale skin.
Black ceremonial clothing that was technically practical and communicated in practice that practicality was entirely beside the point.
Her expression was the absence of expression.
Not the neutrality of a resting face but a specific vacancy, like a room emptied of everything that made it a room.

Behind her in the corridor, the guards.
One of them glanced toward Aife where she stood near the back wall and then away again quickly, the way a man looks away from something he has been told not to look at.
It was there and gone in less than a second, and Denji would not think to question it until far later than he should have.
Beside Kiana, a tall man in dark military clothing.

Long black hair with silver in it.
He wore a black and red mask, covering his face in an ominous yet simple way.
He was positioned in the corridor with the posture of someone present in an official capacity, and he was looking at Denji through the bars with an attention that was entirely different from Kiana's.
Hers was clinical.
His was the look of a man who had come to confirm something he had needed to see for himself.

Kiana looked at the bound arm.
She looked at the state of him generally with the same careful going-over.
"Your wound should have progressed considerably further than this by now," she observed, delivering it with the flatness of someone noting a condition in a controlled environment.
"The infection rate is inconsistent with your timeline."

He gave her nothing.

"Your tissue is not doing what tissue in your condition does."
She looked at him with that particular quality of interest again.
"That is an observation, not a question.
I have sufficient questions already."

She turned and made a small gesture with two fingers.

From somewhere past the edge of the window's frame came a dragging sound, and then the door was unlocked from the outside, and the guards brought Mei into the corridor directly in front of it.

She was standing upright, barely.
The guards had her by both arms and none of the resistance she'd had when they'd moved her through earlier remained.
Whatever had happened in the laboratory levels had taken something from the ratio.
Her eyes were open, fixed on a point between the floor and the middle distance, and her breathing was shallow and fast, the breathing of someone who was concentrating very hard on something that should not require concentration.

Her gaze moved and found Kiana.

*There it was.*

Not the way a prisoner looked at a jailer.
Not even the way a person looked at someone they feared.
It was something older than that, rawer.
A look that had too much history in it.
A look that required years of specific shared experience to produce.
Denji had no name for it yet.
He only knew it didn't fit the room.

"Don't," Mei told her.
Her voice was level.
Steadier than it had any reason to be.
The single word carried something beneath it that was not a plea and was not simply a command.
It was the specific word of someone who had spoken it before, in this corridor or one like it, and had not been listened to, and was speaking it again anyway because staying silent was not something they were capable of.

Kiana's expression did not change.

She reached into the fold of her clothing and produced a small object.
Thin, longer than a finger, made of something dark that did not catch the torchlight as metal or glass would have.
It looked organic.
Shaped gradually rather than made.

The tall man beside her looked away.

Not far.
Not pretending.
He simply turned his head to a point slightly left of the scene in front of him and held it there with the deliberate stillness of a person who had made a decision about what they were going to witness and was executing it precisely.
His breathing was measured.
His hands at his sides were very still.

Denji watched him.

*He knows her,* Denji thought.
*He knows both of them.
He has seen this before.*

Kiana pressed the object once to the side of Mei's neck.

What followed was brief and total.

Mei's body went rigid for one second, two.
Not tension but its opposite, a total arrest of voluntary motion.
Then her head dropped forward.
Then she took a breath that was not hers.
Slower.
Calibrated differently.
As if whatever was now managing the body had its own ideas about the appropriate pace of things.

The air in the corridor changed.

A pressure drop, or the suggestion of one.
A quality to the torchlight that took the definition away from the edges of things.
Denji felt the familiar warmth rise behind his sternum, answering something it recognized as wrong, and he held it.

Mei's head lifted.

Her amber eyes were the same but something behind them was not organized in the same way.
It was her face, her stillness, the particular architecture of her expression.
But there was a layer beneath the surface that was looking at him from much further back than the face.
A depth that was not hers.

*I won't hurt her.*

The thought was simple and it immediately made everything more complicated.

He moved anyway.

He crossed the cell.
He hit the iron door with his right shoulder and it did not move, because it was locked, because it was always locked, and the impact cost him a solid jolt through his ribs.
He got his hands around the bars of the window and pulled.

"Denji," Aife called.

"I know."

"The door-"

"I know."

He held the bars and looked through the window at Mei standing in the corridor three feet away.
She looked back at him.
The thing behind her eyes had decided what it thought of him in the way things without much patience decided quickly.
He watched it reach up through her and settle in her shoulders, her hands, the angle of her chin.

She hit the door.

Both palms, hard, and the force behind it was not hers, it was something that had measured the output it needed and applied it precisely.
The door held.
He stepped back.
He did not think she could take the door off its hinges but he thought she would try for a long time if he stayed in front of it.

He looked past her at the tall man in the corridor.

The tall man had turned back.
He was watching now.
But his attention was on Mei, not on Denji, and the look about him was the look of a man watching something he could not stop and had decided he must simply endure.

Kiana was watching Denji.

She had been watching him the whole time.
She was not watching Mei, or the door, or the guards.
She was watching Denji with the careful, cataloguing quality she had brought through the corridor with her, and it had sharpened since the object had been pressed to Mei's neck.
Since the air had changed and Denji had stayed on his feet and moved toward the door instead of away from it.

The warmth in his sternum had not gone down.
It was still there, pressing outward, held back deliberately, a thing with opinions about what was happening that he was not letting act.
He held it.
He held it and looked through the bars and could not do anything useful and did not look away.

After a time, Kiana made the small gesture again.

The guards took hold of Mei.
She did not fight them.
She walked with them the way someone walked when the person doing the walking had been given a direction and no particular feelings about it.
She disappeared around the turn in the corridor.

Gone.

The tall man stood alone in the corridor.
He looked at Denji through the bars for a long moment.
Then his gaze moved, briefly, to the turn in the corridor where Mei had gone.
Something in it then.
Something he did not allow to stay long.
He turned and walked away.

Kiana was last.

She had not moved.
She stood at the door and looked at Denji with that precisely sharpened interest, and she took her time about it.
He looked back at her and offered nothing, and she appeared to find his silence at least as informative as anything he might have given her.

"He survived contact," she remarked.

Not to him.
She directed it toward the corridor, toward the retreating footsteps, as an observation made in the direction of the only other person in this building whose opinion on the matter she considered worth offering it to.

Then she walked away, and the corridor was empty, and the door was locked, and the torch continued its slow and patient burning.

Aife did not speak immediately.

She tore a strip from the inner lining of her robes and passed it to him without comment.
The binding on his arm had wept through the cloth from where he'd hit the door.
He took it and pressed it over the old cloth and held it there.

"The thing she put in her," he began, eventually.
"Can it be removed?"

Aife was quiet for a moment.
"I don't know," she answered.
"The records I have studied suggest that the Shadow implantation process is meant to be permanent.
But the records I have studied on this subject are incomplete."
She paused.
"Most of what I know about what Kiana does in the lower levels I have assembled from what comes back upstairs."

He sat on the floor with his back to the door.
"That look," he murmured.
"Mei.
The way she looked at Kiana."

"Yes," Aife returned.
A short word carrying more weight than it seemed to want to.

"That was not a prisoner looking at the person running the experiments."

Aife did not answer immediately.
He had the sense she was deciding something.
"No," she answered.
"It was not."
She left it there.

He sat with it.

He thought about the tall man's actions when Mei was taken down the corridor.
The way his head had turned and then turned back.
The look after, at the bend where she had disappeared around.
Whatever was in that look had not been the detachment of an officer observing a routine procedure.
It had been something much more personal than that, pressed down beneath a surface that had gotten very good at pressing things down.

He did not yet have the architecture to put these pieces together correctly, but he stored them anyway.
There was a shape coming.
He could feel the edge of it.

He lay back on his pallet and looked at the ceiling, at the precise mortar lines in the black stone, and he listened to the metallic resonance moving through the air of the Hold.
Long and low and patient.
Coming from somewhere far below.

The warmth in his chest had not gone completely.
It moved slow in there, the way heat moved in stone after the fire was gone, residual and unbothered, and whatever it was had no particular urgency about what was coming.

It had been here before.
It would be here after.
It was, in this way, exactly like the Hold itself, and exactly like the thing the Hold had been built around.

He closed his eyes.

Down the corridor, in the direction they had taken Mei, something moved very quietly through the dark, and whether it was a guard or a Shadow or the building itself settling on its foundations was not a question the dark was willing to answer.
