
The armory sat at the lowest habitable level of the Sungrave Citadel, in a place most soldiers had no reason to visit and fewer reasons to linger.

The stonework changed as one descended toward it. The upper fortress was built of carefully dressed granite, fitted and mortared with the precision of a city that had spent generations deciding what it wanted to look like. Deeper down, that certainty eroded. The stones changed the deeper you went - rougher, older, uneven in ways Denji could never quite explain. Walls a little too thick, corridors a little too low, angles that did not resolve the way angles were supposed to. The air changed too, taking on a mineral cold that seemed to rise from beneath the fortress itself. It was colder the deeper you went, though not in any way tied to the weather.

Lanterns burned at intervals along the outer corridor. Inside the armory itself the light came from a pair of work-lamps positioned close on either side of the central bench, and they made a small warm world of the space between them and left the rest of the room to the dark.

Denji had been there since before the fourth bell.

He worked with his left arm braced across the bench and the damaged gauntlet held steady in his right hand, re-threading a split seam along the inner wrist with a curved needle and waxed cord. The leather had shredded for the second time that month. There was no wound responsible for it - no clean explanation he could point to - only the accumulated stress of something the original armorer had not accounted for, the material failing slowly and then all at once. He pulled each stitch tight with care, working by lamplight, not rushing.

It was careful work. He preferred careful work.

The wall to his left held a long diagonal crack that had opened sometime in the past six months, stuffed with old grey mortar that was itself beginning to separate from the edges. If the lamplight caught it at the right angle, one could see through the gap to the surface behind the facing stone - older, differently textured, curving slightly inward in a way the Citadel's foundation did not. Whatever it was, the builders had simply constructed against it rather than investigating it. Black crystalline threads traced the mortar lines outward from the base of the crack in both directions, thin as wire, faintly reflective, following the seams the way roots followed water.

Denji had stopped registering them weeks ago.

He tied off the stitch and tested the seam between his fingers. It held. He lifted the gauntlet and turned it under the light, checking the articulated plates along the back for the hairline fractures he had catalogued the last time he had done this. Most were unchanged. One near the second knuckle had extended slightly. He set the gauntlet down on the cloth beside him.

It shifted.

Not dramatically. The fingers curled slightly at the tips, like a sleeping hand twitching. Denji looked at it. The gauntlet was still. He looked at his own hand resting on the bench, and then back at the gauntlet, and then he picked up the pauldron and continued working.

---

He heard her footsteps on the lower stair before she appeared: quick and even, entirely unbothered by the architecture, the pace of someone who had decided where she was going and found nothing between here and there worth slowing down for.

Aella came through the doorway with a lantern borrowed from the corridor hook and looked around the armory with the calm, half-amused expression of someone who had already known what they would find and had come anyway.

"There you are," she said.

"I wasn't hiding," Denji said, without looking up.

"You have been down here since the second bell." She set her lantern on the corner of the bench and it added its warmth to the two work-lamps, brightening the room by a small but noticeable degree. "Commander Vael is going to send someone eventually."

"Vael knows where I am."

"Vael has a reasonable suspicion. That is different from knowing." She leaned against the bench beside him and looked at the pieces laid out on the cloth. Her silver-blonde hair was pinned loosely at the nape of her neck, already coming free on one side. She was still in her healer's robes, white-and-gold, the hem worked with a pale embroidered border that was more practical than decorative in ways not immediately obvious. She studied the pauldron in his hands. "The rear edge is warped all the way along the brace."

"Yeah."

"Can it be fixed?"

"There is a forge on the sub-level. It will take time."

"You have always been patient with things that take time," she said, and the lightness in her voice was deliberate, the kind she used when she was leading somewhere. "Is this the third time this week you have been down here, or the fourth?"

"I like the quiet."

"I know that." She picked up the repaired gauntlet and turned it as he had, fingers moving along the seam with the automatic thoroughness of someone who spent most of her hours examining things for hidden damage. "The stitching is clean. You have gotten better at this."

"I have had practice."

She set it down. "Denji."

"I know," he said.

She was quiet after that, which communicated something on its own. Aella stayed quiet for a moment.It appeared that she was deciding whether to press or to wait, turning the question over, measuring what it was worth. Somewhere in the stone around them, the Citadel made its low settling sound, or water moved through some channel too deep to see. The two were difficult to tell apart.

"There is a bird living in the east corridor," she said, after a moment. Her tone had shifted to something easier. "A pigeon. I found it three floors below any window. I have no idea how it arrived there. It was simply sitting on the floor, looking profoundly put-upon."

Denji looked up.

Something in his expression moved, not quite a smile but adjacent to one. "What did you do with it."

"I brought it up to the parapet and released it. It flew approximately four feet, landed on the nearest merlon, and regarded me with what I can only describe as disappointment." She watched him with the patience she reserved for moments when she had successfully shifted something in his face. "I am telling you this so you understand that even the stray wildlife here moves around more than you do when you get like this."

"I am not like anything."

"You are sitting alone in an armory at the fifth bell on a rest-day, repairing equipment that could wait until tomorrow." She reached over and pressed the back of his hand once, briefly. "Come up to the hall. Let Vael see that you are alive. You can come back afterward."

"After the pauldron."

"After the pauldron," she agreed, and she settled against the bench without further argument and fell into a companionable quiet beside him, which was one of the things he valued most about her.

---

The clock stopped at some point while they were talking.

It was a simple iron-cased piece with a white face, hung on the wall between two storage racks, and it had kept reliable time for as long as it had been there. There was no fault in the mechanism that would have explained it. The hands simply ceased to move, arrested at a position slightly before the sixth bell. It hung there, showing the wrong time with absolute composure while the lamps burned and the conversation continued and Denji worked the pauldron between his hands.

Neither of them noticed.

"Do you ever feel as though you have lived the same day before?"

The voice came from the doorway.

Erembour stood just past the threshold, not fully entering and not retreating, his hands at his sides and his dark coat unchanged from that morning. He wasn’t looking at either of them. His attention had fixed on the stopped clock, calm but intent - as if he recognized something in it. Something about the clock clearly unsettled him.

Denji turned on the stool. "What?"

Erembour's gaze moved from the clock to him. His expression resettled into the composed, faintly measured look he typically carried, whatever had been there a moment before erased cleanly. "Good evening," he said, to both of them.

"Good evening," Aella said, carefully.

His eyes moved to the clock once more, briefly, and then returned. "Forgive the intrusion. There is a council convening within the hour." A pause. "The reports from the western parishes have come in."

"What reports," Denji said.

"Troubling ones." He said it without emphasis, as though trouble were simply the expected weather. He took in the room for a moment with an expression that felt less like observation and more like recognition, and then he stepped back into the corridor and his footsteps receded up the stair without haste.

Aella looked at Denji.

Denji looked at the empty doorway.

"He asked you something," she said.

"I noticed."

"Are you going to consider it?"

He did not answer immediately. He set the pauldron down and stood, the stool scraping back across the stone floor. He picked up the repaired gauntlet, turned it once in his hands out of habit, and set it back with the other pieces. "What kind of reports come from the western parishes?"

Aella said nothing, because neither of them needed to answer that.

---

The war chamber sat at the upper tier of the Sungrave Citadel, circular and wide, with narrow windows set into the outer wall at measured intervals and a map-table at its center broad enough that four people standing at the edges could not reach the middle. By the time Denji arrived, the senior commanders were already positioned around the table and the scouts stood at the far end with the still, contained posture of men who had delivered their information and were waiting to learn what it meant to someone with the authority to decide.

The Matrix stood at the head of the table.

He was not a physically imposing man, but the room arranged itself around him nonetheless. There was a quality to how he held himself, something compressed and long-practiced, as though the effort of keeping things together had been going on for so many years it had become indistinguishable from his ordinary bearing. He looked at the reports spread before him with an expression that gave nothing away except that he had read them carefully.

"The western villages," he said. "Summarize."

The first scout stepped forward. "Four settlements in two weeks. Rethavar, the Soll Crossing, Underbend, and the mill district at Hayle's Ford." He paused for a moment before continuing. "Gone is the word we settled on. No bodies recovered from any of them. No fire damage, no structural collapse, no signs of an engagement. The livestock were gone alongside the people. Cook-fires were cold. In one house in Rethavar the table was still set for a meal."

The chamber was silent for a moment.

"No bodies," said Commander Vael, from the left side of the table.

"None."

"Chimera columns do not operate that way," Vael said, addressing the room rather than the scout. "They leave evidence. They have always left evidence."

"That has become a less reliable assumption," said another voice.

Erembour stood at the window to the right of the table, facing the glass, his arms folded behind his back. He had been there when Denji entered and had not shifted position.

"Since the crossing at Colham three months ago, their movements have carried a coherence they did not possess before," he continued, without turning. "A column crossed the river and advanced to within engagement range of Harren's Gate, then halted for two days and withdrew without contact. There was no tactical explanation for the withdrawal - not an obvious one, at least." He turned from the window to face the table. "They were not retreating. They were measuring our response time. How quickly Bright Hill reacted. How many soldiers we moved and from which directions. They were not there to fight. They were there to learn."

"Learn what, exactly," the Matrix said.

"How we answer." Erembour looked at the map. "The disappearances in the west are not raids. They are not punitive actions. They are preparation for something that has not happened yet, and the pattern of it points toward the Hold. The strategic coordination we are seeing is densest in the territory nearest to it." He lifted his gaze to the Matrix. "We should not advance forces into that region. Not until we understand what is beneath it."

"Beneath it," Vael repeated, with the flat tone of a man who had decided to take the statement literally and found it wanting.

"The survivors," Erembour said. "From the western settlements."

The scout nodded. "Five of them, across two villages, with no contact between them before we brought them in. All five gave the same account independently."

"Whispers," Erembour said, before the scout could continue.

The scout looked at him. "Yes, sir. Underground. They all said they heard them coming up through the floors in the days before the others disappeared."

The chamber sat with that for a moment. Several of the commanders made small movements that were not quite movements, adjustments of weight and posture that expressed something they had not put into words. The Matrix looked at the map for a long time, and then he looked at Erembour.

"You are recommending we wait," the Matrix said.

"I am recommending we do not march toward a threat we cannot see, into territory we do not understand, in response to provocations that may have been designed specifically to draw us out." Something in Erembour's voice had changed slightly beneath its even surface. "The Hold was not built where it was built without reason. What lies beneath it is not something that should be approached without-"

"We cannot simply wait." Commander Hadris spoke from the right side of the table, and his voice carried the particular force of a man who had been holding the objection back and had decided to stop. "We cannot leave the western parishes exposed. We cannot tell this city that four villages disappeared without bodies and that we have chosen patience as our answer. The people will not accept that and they should not be asked to."

Other voices followed his, overlapping, agreement threading between the different ranks with the ease of a thing most of the room had already been thinking.

The Matrix raised one hand. The voices fell away.

"I hear it," he said. He regarded Erembour for a moment with an expression that neither dismissed the argument nor adopted it. "We will review the full assessments before any decision is made. The scouts return to the western perimeter. The Colham garrison is reinforced by the end of the week." He looked around the table. "We reconvene in three days."

The council broke apart slowly, conversation quieter than before. Each of them silently agreeing that they had reached a conclusion that none of them were entirely sure was the correct one.

Denji did not move immediately. He watched Erembour across the table.

Erembour had not left the window. He was looking down at the city - the lantern lines of the lower districts, the pale outer walls, the deep foundations cutting through the dark beneath Bright Hill. He didn’t look frustrated. He looked worried

He looked like a man watching something he had already seen. Beneath the composure, Denji caught something akin to fear.

---

Denji went back down to the armory after the council dismissed, yet he did not resume the repair work. He sat on the stool and looked at the wall where the crystalline veins traced their dark paths through the mortar, and at the crack through which something older curved silently away from the Citadel's foundations, and he thought about food left on a table in an empty house, and whispers rising through a floor, and the particular way Erembour had looked at the stopped clock before he had schooled his expression back into something unreadable.

On the far wall, the clock still showed the wrong time.

He had not yet noticed it.
